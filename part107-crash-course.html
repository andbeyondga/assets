Learn this first — half the exam lives here

1 · Read a sectional chart & nail airspace

Roughly a third to a half of the test is airspace and chart reading, and it's where almost everyone bleeds points. Master this and you've basically passed. Everything else is easier.

The only color code you must memorize

Solid blue ring → Class B (big city). Needs ATC auth.
Solid magenta ring → Class C. Needs ATC auth.
Dashed blue ring → Class D. Needs ATC auth.
Dashed magenta ring → Class E to the surface. Needs ATC auth.
Faded magenta band → Class E starting at 700 ft AGL. No auth needed for low drone ops.
No ring (most of the map) → Class G, uncontrolled. No authorization needed.
The decision you'll make over and over on the test:
"Is my drone site in B, C, D, or surface-E?" → You need ATC authorization (get it instantly via LAANC or apply through FAA DroneZone).
"Is it in G, or faded-magenta E?" → Just fly (still obey altitude/VLOS rules).

Two more chart symbols they love to test

  • Airport color: blue airport = has a control tower; magenta airport = no tower.
  • Altitude fractions in a class ring (e.g. 125 / 45) = ceiling 12,500 ft over floor 4,500 ft MSL (hundreds of feet). A floor of SFC means surface.
  • MEF (big faint numbers in each grid square, e.g. 2¹) = highest terrain/obstacle in that quadrant, MSL.
  • Obstruction (tower): bold number = top in MSL, number in (parentheses) = height AGL.

Practical use: given any chart cutout, you can now state the airspace, whether you need authorization, the floor/ceiling, and avoid the tallest obstacle. That's 90% of the chart questions.

One-liners that are basically free questions:
  • The Remote PIC is the final authority — always.
  • Small UAS always yields right-of-way to manned aircraft.
  • One drone per pilot at a time. No hazmat. No careless/reckless ops.
  • You may deviate from any rule in an in-flight emergency (and file a report if asked).
  • FPV alone ≠ VLOS — you or a visual observer must see the aircraft with your own eyes.
Learn third — small, decode-able chunk

3 · Decode a METAR & the 2 weather ideas they test

You don't need meteorology. You need to read one line of code and know two concepts.

Read this METAR left to right

KOKC 011451Z 24008KT 3SM BKN015 12/09 A2992

  • 24008KT → wind from 240° true at 8 knots (gusts shown as G, e.g. 24015G25KT).
  • 3SM → visibility 3 statute miles → exactly your legal minimum.
  • BKN015 → broken clouds at 1,500 ft → that's your ceiling. (FEW→SCT→BKN→OVC = thin → solid.)
  • 12/09 → temp 12°C / dew point 9°C. Close together = fog/low cloud risk.

The only two concepts

  • Density altitude: hot + high + humid = thin air = worse performance (less lift, slower climb, less payload). Temperature matters most.
  • Stable vs unstable air: stable → stratus clouds, steady wind, poor visibility/haze. Unstable → puffy cumulus, turbulence, good visibility, showers.

That's the whole weather section in practice. Skip the textbook.

Completely ignore (for now)

4 · What to NOT waste your 4 hours on

  • Deep meteorology — frontal systems, adiabatic lapse rates, cloud-formation theory. Learn the two concepts above and stop.
  • Manned-aircraft systems & instruments — altimeter settings math, VOR/radio navigation, engine systems. Not your test.
  • Manual weight-and-balance math — for drones it's conceptual ("too heavy / bad CG = worse, less stable"), not a calculation.
  • Memorizing every special-use airspace — just know: Prohibited = never; Restricted = need permission; MOA/Warning/Alert = use caution. Done.
  • Sectional chart minutiae — VOR compass roses, airway routes, frequencies. You're not navigating an airliner.
  • Loading theory, aerodynamics derivations, history of the FAA. Zero questions, infinite time-sink.
If a study resource spends 30 minutes on any bullet above, skip ahead. That time belongs to sectionals.
★ The one exercise that beats 70% of "studiers"

5 · Do this once, today

Most people fail because they read about airspace instead of reading charts. This single drill turns the hardest section into your strongest.

1
Download the FAA's free Airman Knowledge Testing Supplement (CT-8080-2H) — it contains the exact sectional figures used on the real exam. (faa.gov testing supplements)
2
Pick 10 random airports/points across those figures. For each, say out loud and write down:
  • What airspace class is it? (use the color code above)
  • Do I need ATC authorization / LAANC? (yes for B/C/D/surface-E, no for G)
  • What's the floor & ceiling of that airspace?
  • What's the tallest obstacle nearby and its height?
3
Check yourself. Wherever you hesitated, that's your weak airspace type — repeat just those until it's instant.
Why this works: the test reuses those very figures. If you can read them cold, the airspace questions become free points — and that's the swing section between pass and fail.
